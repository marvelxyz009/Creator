The clinic's door had not been re-locked since the closure. Mira pushed it open and the salt-damp came up to meet her, a mix of cold and stillness she had not prepared for. Tomas was already there, seated at the consultation desk by the window. He had a small brass lighter in his hand, and he was turning it once, slowly, the way someone turns a coin to decide which side they had meant.

She did not sit.

"You knew I'd come tonight," she said.

"I hoped you would."

"That isn't the same thing."

He set the lighter down on the edge of the desk. For a moment, neither of them spoke, and Mira felt a wave of unease move through her chest. She crossed to the desk and moved the stapler an inch to the left, into its old alignment.

"Five days ago," she said, "I asked you a direct question. About my record. And you answered with a sentence I have been turning over since."

"I remember the sentence."

"Then you also remember that it wasn't an answer."

He smiled, sadly, and she hated that she noticed. "Mira. There were things, in that period of your life, that—"

"Don't."

"—that I judged it kinder to set aside."

"*Set aside.*"

"It is the word I used at the time."

"It is the word a licensed editor uses on a form."

"I was a licensed editor."

"You weren't licensed for me."

Outside, the wave came in on the stones, and the fluorescent above the desk gave its slow tick. He picked up the lighter again, and she watched his hand without letting her face register that she was watching.

"I set aside a portion of what we shared," he said. "Partly. Within reason."

"Within whose reason?"

"Mine. At the time."

She felt anger rise in her, sharp and sudden, but she did not let it show. There was a piece of cracked glass set into the surface of the desk, an old workplace ornament, and two of the shards had drifted out of alignment. She fitted them back together with the side of her thumb, mid-sentence.

"What do you think I am supposed to do with that," she said, "now that you've told me."

"I'm not sure you needed to know even that much."

"I have been carrying a grief I cannot source, Tomas. For three years. I have been waking at four in the morning with a feeling that has no file attached to it. Do you know what that is like?"

"I have an idea."

"You have more than an idea."

He looked at her for a long moment, and then he reached, almost absently, and straightened the stapler she had moved.

She nearly laughed. The laugh did not come. "You can't help it," she said. "You see something off and you fix it."

"It's a habit."

"It's a tell."

"Mira—"

"Did you ask me first." It was not a question by then; she could not lift it into one. "Before you set anything aside. Did you ask."

"I asked you something. I'm not sure what I asked counts."

"That's the most honest sentence you have said since I walked in."

"I know."

The room held its small silence. The hum of the dead refrigeration unit, the wave, the tick of the fluorescent: she counted them like she was counting reasons to stay or to go.

She reached across the desk and picked up the lighter.

It was warm from his hand. She turned it once, the way he had — and stopped. Her thumb had gone to the wheel from the side, not the top; her index had braced the base where it was worn. She struck it once. The flame came up clean on the first try.

She looked at the flame, not at him.

"I have never seen this lighter before," she said.

"No."

"My record does not contain a single instance of me seeing this lighter."

"No."

"Then explain to me how my hand knows where the worn part is."

He did not answer. She would not have let him.

She set the lighter back on the desk, exactly where it had been, the angle exactly as he had left it. She did not say goodbye. She did not look at him as she crossed to the door. Behind her, very quietly, he said something that might have been her name and might have been a fragment of a longer word he had decided, too late, not to say.

She walked out into the salt and the wave.
